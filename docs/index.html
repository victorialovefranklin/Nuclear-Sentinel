Nuclear Sentinel

Nuclear Sentinel is a digital-twin-enabled cybersecurity monitoring and decision-support research framework that integrates control/command evidence, physical-process measurements, expected-versus-observed digital-twin behavior, operating context, and cyber-physical interpretation. Experiments 1–3 evaluate the detection and digital-twin-context layers using public ICS/CPS research environments; dynamic risk and response/recovery remain broader framework layers requiring additional validation.

Nuclear Sentinel · Literature Survey & Critical Review

Digital-Twin-Enabled Cyber-Physical Monitoring for Nuclear Operational Technology

Survey focus. This literature survey examines how researchers monitor and interpret unusual behavior in industrial and nuclear operational technology. It focuses on control and command information, physical sensor measurements, digital twins, anomaly detection, explainability, calibration, changing operating conditions, cyber-physical risk, and human-supervised decision support. The purpose is to understand what the research already shows, where researchers disagree, what limitations remain, and what should be studied next.

Scope. The survey studies the broader research field first. Nuclear Sentinel is used only to show how the identified research gaps connect to this dissertation. The literature is not organized to prove that Nuclear Sentinel is correct. Instead, the framework is positioned only after the strengths, weaknesses, tradeoffs, and open problems in existing research are examined.

Ethical, Legal & Professional Considerations

Monitoring critical infrastructure requires more than high detection accuracy. Research should consider whether data are collected and used appropriately, whether sensitive system information is protected, whether explanations are understandable to the people responsible for decisions, and whether automated outputs could be mistaken for confirmed attacks or safety conclusions. For Nuclear Sentinel, human oversight is therefore treated as a design requirement rather than an optional feature.

The survey will also distinguish cybersecurity research evidence from formal nuclear safety or regulatory conclusions. A research anomaly score, digital-twin residual, or machine-learning alert should not be presented as proof of a cyberattack, equipment failure, or nuclear safety condition without additional evidence and qualified human review.

Strengths, Limitations & Tradeoffs

The survey will compare not only which methods report strong results, but also what those results cost. A detector may find more attacks while creating more false alarms. A digital twin may provide useful physical context while requiring reliable models and calibration. A more complex multimodal system may provide richer evidence while becoming harder to maintain or explain. These tradeoffs are central to deciding whether a method is useful beyond a single experiment.

Contradictions, Disagreements & Assumptions to Test

A PhD-level survey should not force the literature into a single story. The final review will explicitly record cases where studies reach different conclusions or rely on different assumptions. Important comparison questions include whether more evidence actually improves detection, whether digital-twin deviations remain reliable after the environment changes, whether higher recall creates an unacceptable false-alarm cost, whether explanations are useful to operators rather than only to model developers, and whether results from laboratory testbeds can reasonably transfer to safety-critical operational settings.

These questions will be answered from the retained literature. The dashboard will distinguish a published finding from an interpretation and from a dissertation research question so that disagreement is not presented as settled fact.

Open Problems

Open problems to be tested against the literature include cross-environment calibration, operating-state-aware thresholds, reliable interpretation of digital-twin residuals, multimodal evidence fusion without excessive false alarms, explanations that connect alerts to physical process meaning, and the connection between anomaly evidence and consequence-aware human decision support. A topic will be labeled a research gap only when the completed literature mapping supports that conclusion.

Research Gap

The survey does not assume that machine learning, digital twins, explainability, multimodal monitoring, or cyber-physical risk analysis are missing from the field. These capabilities already exist in different forms. The research question is whether they have been sufficiently connected and experimentally evaluated across the full path from control and physical-process evidence, through digital-twin comparison and system-state understanding, to consequence-aware risk interpretation and human-supervised resilience decisions.

The working gap for the survey is therefore an integration and validation gap: existing research provides many of the individual building blocks, but the literature must be examined to determine where evidence remains limited for connecting those building blocks across environments, operating conditions, calibration settings, and decision-support needs.

Future Research Directions

Future work should follow directly from gaps established by the literature rather than from general statements that “more research is needed.” Priority questions include how digital-twin residuals should be calibrated when systems change, how operating state should affect alert thresholds, how multiple evidence sources should be combined without creating unnecessary false alarms, how explanations can be translated into useful operator information, and how detection evidence can be connected to consequence and resilience decisions without replacing qualified human judgment.

Research questions

Conclusion

Experiment 1 establishes that control/command and physical-process evidence can support unusual-system recognition and that DT residual context can materially change detection behavior. Expected-versus-observed physical behavior can contribute useful information beyond the combined raw control and process measurements under the HAI/HAIEnd conditions.

The effect was not consistent across algorithms. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting did not reproduce the same magnitude or direction of improvement, which limits any claim that DT context is universally beneficial. That model dependence directly motivated Experiment 2: an external evaluation was required to determine whether the apparent DT benefit would transfer to a different industrial computer-controlled physical environment.

Research questions

Conclusion

Experiment 2 demonstrates that the experimental architecture can be transferred to a second ICS/CPS environment, but the detection behavior does not transfer uniformly. Threshold-sensitivity and normal-data-change analyses show that the apparent value of DT context depends strongly on how the detector is calibrated and on the environment to which it is applied.

This does not negate Experiment 1. Instead, it identifies a more important scientific limitation: DT residual information can be informative while still being poorly calibrated for a new operating environment. Experiment 3 was therefore designed as a targeted follow-on study of adaptive calibration, drift, operating state, residual transfer, setting thresholds with normal data from the new environment, and hybrid combining different types of evidence.

Research questions

Conclusion

Experiment 3 shows that the DT residual problem observed during external validation is substantially a calibration-transfer problem. Source-normal residual thresholds did not transfer cleanly to the SWaT evaluation environment. Setting thresholds with normal data from the new environment changed the residual thresholds substantially for several channels and sharply reduced normal exceedance for those channels. This improves the interpretability of DT deviations, but the remaining normal elevated-deviation rate means DT evidence should still not be treated as a standalone attack indicator.

The A5 hybrid demonstrates the value of combining evidence rather than relying on a single DT alarm. Its Macro-F1 remained statistically similar to fixed M1, while the A5−A0 bootstrap interval was positive. Operating-state results further show that detector behavior changes with system context. Together, these findings support aware of operating conditions, calibrated, human-supervised interpretation within Nuclear Sentinel.

Research Interpretation

Strengths & Limitations

The Nuclear Sentinel experimental program provides a coherent progression from primary evaluation to external validation and then to calibration- and aware of operating conditions refinement. Its principal strength is not that digital-twin context improves every model or metric. Rather, the experiments identify when digital-twin information contributes useful unusual-system evidence, when that contribution fails to transfer cleanly across environments, and how calibration, operating context, and combining different types of evidence affect the resulting detection tradeoffs.

The results should therefore be interpreted as validation of selected computational components of the Nuclear Sentinel framework under public ICS/CPS research conditions. Experiments 1–3 provide evidence for unusual-system detection, digital-twin context, whether thresholds set in one environment still work in another, data-change analysis, differences across operating conditions, and hybrid combining different types of evidence. They do not constitute testing in a real operating environment in a nuclear facility, nor do they yet validate the complete dynamic-risk, human decision-support, response, or recovery layers of the framework.

Results Strengths

1. Coherent three-experiment progression

Experiment 1 establishes whether digital-twin context can contribute useful information. Experiment 2 tests whether that behavior transfers to a second industrial computer-controlled physical environment. Experiment 3 directly investigates the calibration, data-change, operating-state, and false-positive limitations identified during external validation. This progression creates an evidence trail rather than three disconnected model evaluations.

2. Strong primary digital-twin result

In the HAI/HAIEnd Isolation Forest evaluation, M1 increased Macro-F1 from approximately 0.502 to 0.640. True positives increased from 135 to 1,553 while false positives decreased from 1,590 to 511. This provides clear evidence that digital-twin residual context can add useful unusual-system information under some tested conditions.

3. External validation exposes transfer behavior

The framework was not evaluated on HAI/HAIEnd alone. Applying the same B1/B2/B3/M1 progression to SWaT showed that the value of digital-twin context changes across environments. M1 increased attack recall and reduced detection latency, while also increasing the number of false alarms at the primary threshold. This is an important generalizability finding rather than a result to conceal.

4. Calibration and change in the data pattern are investigated explicitly

The research moves beyond reporting that external performance changed. Threshold-sensitivity, normal-score data-change, residual-transfer, and setting thresholds with normal data from the new environment analyses investigate why the behavior changed and demonstrate that where and how the thresholds were set materially affects digital-twin evidence.

5. Hybrid evidence reduces number of false alarms

In Experiment 3, A5 reduced false positives from 2,014 under fixed M1 to 1,483, a reduction of 531 false positives or approximately 26%. Attack precision increased from approximately 32.75% to 37.82%, while recall decreased from approximately 14.45% to 13.28%. The strength is therefore an improved precision/false-alarm tradeoff rather than universal superiority across every metric.

6. Mixed findings are reported transparently

Digital-twin context did not produce the same effect across algorithms, environments, thresholds, or operating states. Reporting those differences strengthens the scientific interpretation because Nuclear Sentinel is being evaluated as a contextual computer-controlled physical framework rather than presented as a universally superior detector.

Research Limitations

1. Proxy ICS/CPS environments

HAI/HAIEnd and SWaT are public industrial control and computer-controlled physical research environments. They are not datasets collected from operating nuclear facilities. The experiments therefore validate computational components of Nuclear Sentinel under controlled proxy conditions and should not be described as operational nuclear validation.

2. Cross-environment performance is not uniform

The strong Isolation Forest M1 result observed in Experiment 1 did not transfer directly to SWaT. In Experiment 2, M1 increased recall from approximately 72.23% to 80.80% and reduced median detection latency from 333 seconds to 40 seconds, but FPR increased from approximately 1.85% to 21.09% at the primary threshold. This demonstrates environment- and calibration-dependent behavior.

3. Attack recall remains limited in Experiment 3

Although the A5 hybrid reduced false positives and improved precision relative to fixed M1, its attack recall was approximately 13.28% in the tested configuration. This is low in absolute terms and requires additional improvement before the approach could be considered for operational application.

4. Digital-twin residuals are not attack proof

A large residual indicates that observed physical behavior differs from the digital twin's expected behavior. That deviation may arise from attacks, operating changes, change in the data pattern, modeling error, equipment behavior, or other conditions. Residuals must therefore be interpreted as supporting evidence that must be interpreted with other information rather than standalone proof of cyber compromise.

5. Setting thresholds with normal data from the new environment requires a trusted reference period

Experiment 3 demonstrates that setting thresholds with normal data from the new environment improves residual transfer, but the method assumes access to a known-normal commissioning or reference segment in the target environment. A future operational implementation would require a defensible process for establishing and maintaining that reference behavior.

6. Operating-state and drift models remain limited

The operating-state analysis uses a simplified two-state representation and does not reproduce the full range of nuclear operating modes, transitions, maintenance conditions, or abnormal procedures. Drift-aware threshold switching was also rarely activated in the current experiment, limiting conclusions about its broader effectiveness.

7. Human decision support and dynamic risk remain future validation layers

Experiments 1–3 evaluate computational detection and supporting evidence that must be interpreted with other information. They do not establish that Nuclear Sentinel improves operator decision quality, response time, recovery performance, or nuclear-facility resilience. The dynamic computer-controlled physical risk and response/recovery layers remain framework components requiring subsequent validation.

Overall Interpretation

Taken together, the experiments support a conditional rather than universal conclusion. Digital-twin context provides measurable information for recognizing abnormal computer-controlled physical behavior, but its contribution depends on the detection algorithm, operating environment, calibration strategy, decision threshold, and operating state. The external-validation results show that fixed residual and anomaly thresholds should not be assumed to transfer reliably between environments. Experiment 3 further demonstrates that target-environment calibration and contextual combining different types of evidence can reduce part of the resulting number of false alarms, while also revealing remaining limitations in recall and ability of residuals to distinguish unusual behavior from normal behavior.

These findings support the central design principle of Nuclear Sentinel: digital-twin deviation should be interpreted together with control-system evidence, physical-process measurements, where and how the thresholds were set, operating context, and data-change information. The current evidence therefore supports the detection, digital-twin context, calibration, and evidence-fusion components of the framework. Operational nuclear validation, dynamic computer-controlled physical risk validation, and evaluation of human-supervised response and resilience remain subsequent research stages.
